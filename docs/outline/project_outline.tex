\section{Use Case}
% ============================================================

This project aims to build a unified view of approved pharmaceutical drugs in the United
States, the European Union and Canada, enabling researchers and analysts to query regulatory
status, approval history, and product attributes across these markets from a single integrated data set. To achieve this, we combine data from three complementary sources.
Drugs@FDA is the FDA's official database of US-approved drugs. EMA Medicines is
the European Medicines Agency's register of EU-authorised medicines. The Health
Canada Drug Product Database (DPD) contains current and historical drug products from
Health Canada. The central entity is a medicine, represented by its brand name and active
substance or combination of active substances. Each integrated record includes the licence
holder, regulatory status, product class, ATC code, dosage form, route, strengths, active
substance count, regional regulatory dates and generic, biosimilar or orphan flags.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Drugs@FDA}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Drugs@FDA contains 51,839 product rows across 29,377 applications, forming 8,175 normalized medicine entities. Among applications, 78.3\% are ANDAs, 20.1\% NDAs, and 1.7\% BLAs. Three attributes exceed 30\% missingness: \texttt{ApplPublicNotes} (99.99\%), \texttt{SubmissionsPublicNotes} (99.83\%), and \texttt{ReviewPriority} (41.73\%).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{EMA Medicines}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
EMA Medicines contains 2,746 medicine rows, forming 2,620 normalized medicine entities, with no duplicate input rows. Human medicines account for 85.6\% of the records. By status, 68.5\% are authorised, 15.4\% withdrawn, and 10.4\% are withdrawn applications. Twelve attributes exceed 30\% missingness, mostly because veterinary fields do not apply to human medicines and regulatory-event dates apply only to particular statuses.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Health Canada Drug Product Database (DPD)}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
The Health Canada DPD contains 58,137 product records across 12 tables linked by \texttt{DRUG\_CODE}. They are consolidated into 42,042 medicine rows, forming 41,636 normalized medicine entities. Human products account for 87.7\% of the records. By status, 66.5\% are cancelled and 23.0\% marketed. Notable missingness includes ATC code (21.47\%) and first marketed date (11.67\%).

\subsection{Entity Identification and Count Requirement}
% ============================================================

A medicine is identified by its \texttt{brandName} and \texttt{activeSubstances}. Contrary
to initial assumptions, \texttt{licenceHolder} cannot be used as an identifying attribute
because the same medicine is frequently marketed by different regional subsidiaries or
unrelated licensing partners across jurisdictions (for example, \textit{Verkazia} is held
by \texttt{HARROW EYE} in the US, \texttt{Santen Oy} in the EU, and \texttt{APOTEX INC} in
Canada).

To estimate how many medicines appear in multiple datasets based on exact matching, we build
a normalized key (\texttt{medicine\_id}) from \texttt{brandName} and \texttt{activeSubstances}
by converting text to upper case, removing accents and dosage strengths, standardizing
punctuation, and sorting multiple active substances. Let $F$, $E$ and $D$ denote the sets of
\texttt{medicine\_id} values in Drugs@FDA, EMA Medicines and the DPD. The pairwise exact
overlaps are $|F \cap E| = 428$, $|F \cap D| = 949$ and $|E \cap D| = 594$, yielding a
deduplicated union of $\left|(F \cap E) \cup (F \cap D) \cup (E \cap D)\right| = 1{,}407$
distinct medicines -- well above the required minimum of 1,000.

However, exact matching misses many identical medicines because both identifying attributes
can vary across countries (Table~\ref{tab:difficult_match}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Active substance naming and salt forms:} The same substance can have different
  regional spellings (e.g., \texttt{CYCLOSPORINE} in the US and Canada vs.\
  \texttt{ciclosporin} in the EU for \textit{Verkazia}) or different chemical and salt
  representations (e.g., \texttt{GLYCOPYRROLATE} in the US, \texttt{glycopyrronium bromide}
  in the EU, and \texttt{GLYCOPYRRONIUM (GLYCOPYRRONIUM BROMIDE)} in Canada for
  \textit{Seebri}).
  \item \textbf{Brand name suffixes:} The same medicine may carry different device or
  formulation suffixes across markets, such as \texttt{SEEBRI NEOHALER} in Drugs@FDA versus
  \texttt{Seebri Breezhaler} in EMA Medicines and the DPD.
\end{itemize}
